Chapter 01 · Foundation

The mathematics you need to reason with data

Open Lab 1 →

Statistics uses mathematics as a language. The goal of this chapter is not to memorize isolated rules; it is to build enough fluency to recognize quantities, rearrange relationships, read graphs, and reason about uncertain events.

Learning objectives

01 · Represent quantities

Move confidently among fractions, decimals, percentages, ratios, and scientific notation.

02 · Manipulate relationships

Use order of operations and algebra to evaluate and rearrange statistical formulas.

03 · Read functions

Interpret inputs, outputs, slope, intercepts, and graphs in statistical contexts.

04 · Reason about chance

Describe sample spaces and calculate probabilities for simple events and complements.

1.1 Fractions, percentages, and exponents

Fractions and proportions

A fraction represents a part relative to a whole. A proportion states that two ratios are equal.

\[ \frac{a}{b}=\frac{c}{d} \]

Cross-multiplication gives \(ad=bc\), which lets us solve for an unknown quantity.

Statistical example

In a sample of 80 observations, 28 belong to category A. The sample proportion is

\[ \hat p=\frac{28}{80}=0.35. \]

This can be reported as a decimal, \(0.35\), or a percentage, \(35\%\).

Percentages and percentage change

To convert a proportion to a percentage, multiply by 100. Percentage change compares the change to the original value:

\[ \text{Percentage change}=\frac{\text{new}-\text{original}}{\text{original}}\times100\%. \]

If a group mean increases from 40 to 46, the absolute increase is 6 and the percentage increase is

\[ \frac{46-40}{40}\times100\%=15\%. \]

An increase of 6 points and an increase of 15% describe the same change using different scales.

Exponents and roots

Exponents describe repeated multiplication. Roots reverse exponentiation.

\(a^m a^n=a^{m+n}\)    ·    \(\frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}\)    ·    \((a^m)^n=a^{mn}\)    ·    \(a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}\)

Square roots appear throughout statistics. For example, the standard error of a mean is

\[ SE_{\bar x}=\frac{s}{\sqrt n}. \]

When \(s=12\) and \(n=36\), \(SE=12/6=2\).

Check 1.1: A sample contains 45 successes among 120 observations. What percentage are successes?

\(\hat p=45/120=0.375\). Multiplying by 100 gives 37.5%.

1.2 Basic algebra

Order of operations

Evaluate expressions in this order: grouping symbols, exponents, multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction.

For \(3+2(5-1)^2\):

\[ 3+2(4)^2=3+2(16)=35. \]

Solving equations

An equation stays balanced when the same valid operation is applied to both sides.

Solve for an unknown mean

Suppose the standardized score is \(z=1.5\), the raw score is \(x=86\), and the standard deviation is \(s=8\):

\[ z=\frac{x-\bar x}{s}. \]

Substitute and solve:

\[ 1.5=\frac{86-\bar x}{8} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 12=86-\bar x \quad\Rightarrow\quad \bar x=74. \]

Summation notation

The symbol \(\sum\) tells us to add a sequence of values. For observations \(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\),

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i=x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n. \]

The sample mean is

\[ \bar x=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i. \]

For scores \(4,7,8,11\), the mean is \((4+7+8+11)/4=7.5\).

Check 1.2: Rearrange \(SE=s/\sqrt n\) to solve for \(n\).

Multiply by \(\sqrt n\), divide by \(SE\), and square both sides:

\[ \sqrt n=\frac{s}{SE} \quad\Rightarrow\quad n=\left(\frac{s}{SE}\right)^2. \]

1.3 Functions and graphs

A function maps each permitted input to exactly one output. We write \(y=f(x)\).

Linear functions

The equation

\[ y=\beta_0+\beta_1x \]

describes a straight line. The intercept \(\beta_0\) is the expected value of \(y\) when \(x=0\). The slope \(\beta_1\) is the expected change in \(y\) for a one-unit increase in \(x\).

Interpreting a model

For \(\widehat{Score}=62+3.5(Hours)\):

  • The predicted score at zero study hours is 62.
  • Each additional study hour is associated with a 3.5-point increase in the predicted score.
  • At 4 hours, the predicted score is \(62+3.5(4)=76\).

Reading a graph

Before interpreting a graph, ask:

  1. What quantities are on the axes?
  2. What are their units and scales?
  3. Does the graph show individual observations, summaries, or model predictions?
  4. Is the relationship increasing, decreasing, curved, clustered, or interrupted by outliers?
  5. Does the graph support description, prediction, or a causal conclusion?
Check 1.3: In \(\hat y=18-0.7x\), what does the slope mean?

For every one-unit increase in \(x\), the predicted value of \(y\) decreases by 0.7 units. The negative sign indicates the direction.

1.4 Introduction to probability

Probability quantifies uncertainty on a scale from 0 to 1.

\[ 0\leq P(A)\leq1. \]

The sample space contains every possible outcome. An event is a collection of outcomes within that sample space.

1.4.1 Coin flips

For one fair coin flip, \(S=\{H,T\}\), and

\[ P(H)=P(T)=\frac12. \]

For two independent fair flips, \(S=\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}\). The probability of exactly one head is

\[ P(HT\text{ or }TH)=\frac24=\frac12. \]

1.4.2 Simple events

The complement \(A^c\) contains outcomes where \(A\) does not occur:

\[ P(A^c)=1-P(A). \]

For mutually exclusive events \(A\) and \(B\):

\[ P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B). \]

For independent events:

\[ P(A\text{ and }B)=P(A)P(B). \]

Use the complement

If the probability that an instrument fails on a given trial is \(0.03\), the probability it does not fail is

\[ 1-0.03=0.97. \]

Check 1.4: What is the probability of at least one head in two fair coin flips?

Use the complement. The only outcome with no heads is \(TT\), which has probability \(1/4\):

\[ P(\text{at least one head})=1-P(TT)=1-\frac14=\frac34. \]
